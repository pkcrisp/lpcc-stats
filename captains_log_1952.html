🗓️ 27 Sep – 03 Oct 1952 · 1952 — 1 match

Sun 28 Sep 1952 🧢 Marriedv Linton Park CC · HomeformL

Sunday 28 September, and Linton Park travelled with hope in our hearts and, as ever, not quite enough jumpers between us for the weather. Winning the toss, I had no hesitation in sending us in to bat — a decision that looked rather better once Stan Wratten got going. Stan played an innings of real substance, 79 to his name, striking the ball with the sort of authority that makes the rest of us quietly reconsider our own techniques. John Ames matched him for class with a tidy 55, and Gordon Fenner chipped in with 20, as we motored to 35 for the loss of a single wicket in just six overs — brisk work by anyone's standards.

There was a personal landmark buried in all that scoring, too: Stan's 79 eased him past John Thirkell into second on the club's all-time run charts, a bit of history made between overs while most of us were still doing up our pads. He now sits proudly atop the 1952 Married run charts as well, while John Thirkell continues to lead the Singles table — the two of them waging their own quiet contest within the wider one.

In the end neither side could force a result, and honours were shared. A draw, then, but a thoroughly agreeable one — bowled along by a wonderful lesson in batting from Wratten and Ames, and quite possibly the most cheerful stalemate Linton Park has enjoyed all season.

Sun 21 Sep 1952 🧢 1st XIv HC Hayle Place CC · HomeformLDLLL

Sunday last, under a sky that couldn't quite make up its mind, we welcomed Hayle Place CC to the ground, and I won the toss and chose to field first—a decision I stood by right up until Ashby got his eye in. He top-scored with a watchful 43, with useful support from Gosling (27) and Ewell (20), and their total crept to 155 all out from 52.1 overs. It could have been worse: Gordon Fenner steamed in for a fine 4-34, Frank Taylor bowled with real discipline for 2-46 (eight maidens tells its own story), and I chipped in with 1-21 for good measure. Stinchcombe and Pilbeam both departed without troubling the scorers.

Our reply never quite found its rhythm. William Buck's 29 and Don Hartridge's 28 gave us something to build on, and Frank Taylor's useful 20 kept the middle order ticking, but Richard Harris fell for a duck and we stalled at 131 for 7 from our 33 overs. Tony Levick had a torrid time behind the stumps, nine byes going begging.

There were consolations amid the stalemate: Fenner's 17 runs nudged him past Tom Osborn into 17th on the club's all-time run-scoring list, while Taylor's two wickets vaulted him past Fenner into top spot among our all-time wicket-takers—and for what it's worth I'm still leading the 1952 run charts with 773. A draw, then: honours shared, pride largely intact, and the tea urn, as ever, the real winner of the day.

🗓️ 13 Sep – 19 Sep 1952 · 1952 — 2 matches

Sun 14 Sep 1952 🧢 1st XIv MT Medway Towns · HomeformDDLDL

John Thirkell's Verdict — Sunday 14 September 1952

We lost the toss, were sent in to field, and I confess I had that familiar sinking feeling as Medway Towns' G. Lukehurst set about compiling a fine 62. But cricket, as ever, has a way of rewarding patience, and once the wickets began to tumble they came in a heap — Medway all out for 122 off 42 overs, and F. Dixon dispatched without so much as a bead of sweat on the scorers' brows. The star of the show was undoubtedly our Taylor, who spun a web through the middle order for a magnificent 5 for 45; John Ames (3-44) and Frank Taylor (2-7) mopped up admirably, while Tony Snell bowled with the miserly discipline of a man guarding the church collection plate, four maidens and not a run given away lightly.

Chasing 123, I found myself at the crease longer than my knees would have liked, but 52 not out came together nicely, with William Buck (32) and Stan Wratten (22) providing sturdy company — our fourth-wicket stand of 67 was very much the innings' backbone. We closed on 127 for 4 from 30.1 overs, and I'll admit a personal contribution of 52 was satisfying, doubly so for nudging past Stan Wratten into second on the club's all-time run-scorers list — while our Taylor's five-for lifted him past Herbert Rayner into fourth among the wicket-takers, with 52 already banked for the season.

For all that, we were beaten by six wickets in the end. A curious sort of defeat, all things considered — plenty to be proud of, precious little to show for it. We'll be better for it next week.

I made 38 alongside him, and somewhere in there brought up 2,000 career runs for the club — a milestone I shall enjoy rather more in the retelling than I did at the crease. We closed on 176/6, and yet, in one of those quirks the scorebook insists on, we finished on the wrong side of it, beaten by four wickets.

Taylor now leads our 1952 wicket charts with 47, and I'm out in front with the bat on 720 for the season. Small consolations. Heads up, back to the nets, and better luck with the coin next Saturday.

Saturday found us at Yalding, sun doing its best, and the coin obliging me by landing the right way up — I had no hesitation in taking first strike on what looked a shirt-front of a pitch. Some days everything comes off, and this was one of them: I found myself in the sort of form that makes a captain's job embarrassingly easy, and by the close of our innings a rather startled scorer had me down for 120, the bulk of a total of 215 for 3 off our 39 overs. D Simmons chipped in nobly with 43 and Basil Sears added a brisk 23, but it was the second-wicket stand of 152 that really did the damage to Yalding's afternoon.

Defending that lot ought to have been a formality, and for a while it looked it — Yalding lost an early wicket with just 2 on the board and never quite shook off that nervy start, though W Norris (44) and A Stanley (37), with F Hooker's 21, rallied to a third-wicket stand of 66. J Sedge and S Barden departed without troubling the scorers, undone by Frank Taylor, who bowled beautifully for 4 for 27 — enough, we discovered afterwards, to carry him past 200 career wickets and above George Champion to the very top of the club's all-time list. Gerald Wright was no less parsimonious, 3 for 20 with five maidens showing just how little width either man offered. Eight byes told their own tale of a testing day behind the stumps, and Yalding closed on 117 for 7.

Honours shared in the end, then — though with 682 runs now to my name this season and Frank's wicket-taking going from strength to strength, I shan't complain too loudly about a draw.
