🗓️ 26 Sep – 02 Oct 1953 · 1953 — 1 match

Sun 27 Sep 1953 🧢 Marriedv Linton Park CC · HomeformWD

Sunday, 27 September 1953, and Linton Park travelled with hope in our hearts and, in my case at least, precious little else in the runs department.

Winning the toss, I had no hesitation in electing to bat — the pitch looked a belter, and I still maintain it was the right call. What followed was, shall we say, a chastening afternoon for the top order. We were bowled out for a total that has since become the stuff of gazette legend: nought all told, redeemed only by William Buck's gritty unbeaten 26, an innings that now sits proudly atop our 1953 Married run charts. Everyone else, myself included, contributed rather less. I walked back for a duck, and Tony Tipples joined me in that most exclusive of clubs — the pair of us waddling off together in what the ledger cruelly records as the walk of shame. I shall spare the reader further detail on my own effort; suffice to say the scorebook tells the sorry tale well enough without my embellishment.

And yet — cricket has a wonderful habit of humbling everyone equally, for the opposition could find no way through to force a result either. Honours shared, then, a draw when neither side had the stomach to finish the job.

A funny old game: we batted like ducks, bowled like it mattered, and left with pride patched up rather than dented. I'll take a draw after that top order — just don't ask me to explain the details of my own innings again.

🗓️ 19 Sep – 25 Sep 1953 · 1953 — 2 matches

Sun 20 Sep 1953 🧢 1st XIv HC Hayle Place CC · HomeformWDWWW

Skipper's Log — Sunday 20 September 1953

Won the toss, and there really was only one call to make: bat. On a day when 185 felt like it might just be enough, Tony Levick played the innings of the afternoon, 63 not out and unbeaten at the close, with Stan Wratten giving him fine company for a well-made fifty of his own. 185/6 from our 48 overs seemed a total to be proud of as we walked off for tea.

And for a while it looked like it might be. E Watson, G Ashby and L Brunger trooped back without so much as a run to show for it, and Tony Snell was in menacing form — 3 for 8, with nine maidens that told their own story of a bowler in complete command. Alan Wilkes chipped in handsomely with 3 for 27, and Michael Morton with 2 for 3, and between them they had the opposition all out for 58. Poor Tony Levick had a torrid time of it behind the stumps, mind, with ten byes going begging — the one blemish on an otherwise gleaming afternoon.

There was cause for real celebration too: Alan Wilkes brought up his fiftieth career wicket, and both he and Levick moved up the club's all-time lists in the same match. Quite how, with all that in our favour, we contrived to lose by 127 runs is a mystery I shall be pondering over my tea and toast this week. Chin up, lads — we'll be better for it next Sunday.

Sat 19 Sep 1953 🧢 1st XIv Bearsted CC · HomeformLWDWW

The toss deserted us on a fine Saturday, 19 September 1953, as Bearsted CC arrived at Linton Park and promptly asked us to bowl. Not that we minded much — there's a particular pleasure in watching the opposition dig their own grave, and dig they did. From a promising platform they slumped from 57 for 3 to 61 for 6 in the time it takes to pour a tea, with David Sayer, Gordon Hawkins and Mick Gibson all departing without troubling the scorers. Only 'Tony' Snook, with a defiant 25, kept the total respectable. Alan Wilkes was simply unplayable, running through them for 8 for 39, while Gerald Wright bowled with the miserliness of a man who'd rather die than concede a run — seven maidens and 2 for 25 to show for it. Bearsted were bowled out for 72 in 27.3 overs, and we fancied our chances.

Chasing was never going to be straightforward for this side, and so it proved. Tom Osborn's 25 was the innings' backbone, and I was glad to lend a hand with 21 of my own, but from 72 for 4 we somehow contrived to slide to 76 for 7 — Richard Harris, Alan Wilkes and Gerald Wright all making the long walk back for ducks, a fine irony given Wilkes' heroics with the ball. We scrambled home all out for 80 in 28.2 overs, hearts in mouths throughout.

Untidy, unconvincing, but two points banked all the same — and on the day, that's all that counts.

🗓️ 12 Sep – 18 Sep 1953 · 1953 — 2 matches

Sun 13 Sep 1953 🧢 1st XIv MT Medway Towns · HomeformWLWDW

We welcomed Medway Towns for a Sunday fixture on 13 September, and true to recent form I lost the toss — I sometimes wonder if that coin has it in for me — so out we went to field first. It took 43.1 patient overs to have them contained at 173/7, G. Lukehurst leading the way with a fluent 49, K. Walker unbeaten on 48, and R. Harris chipping in with 30. W. Cleaves departed for a duck, undone by Stan Wratten's superb 2-3, with good support from Gordon Fenner's 2-24 and John Ames's 2-46, while Alan Wilkes kept the runs honest with three tidy maidens.

We replied in some style, reaching 176/4 in just 31.3 overs. I was delighted to lead from the front with 51 not out, well supported by Tom Osborn's 46 and William Buck's 35. Poor William Blight had a torrid afternoon behind the stumps, with 9 byes slipping past him, but no matter — the runs were there regardless.

Two footnotes for the record books: Osborn's 46 lifts him past the Rev F A Leveson-Gower into 18th on our all-time run-scorers list, and Wilkes's solitary wicket edges him past W H Fryer into 23rd among the wicket-takers — both still junior, mind, alongside William Buck's 856 and Wilkes's own 39, which lead this season's run and wicket charts respectively.

A tidy win in the end, six wickets and overs to spare — the tea urn, for once, was drunk in celebration rather than consolation.

Sat 12 Sep 1953 🧢 1st XIv YC Yalding CC · HomeformLWLWD

Right then...

I'll admit the toss going against us on Saturday didn't fill me with confidence, but as it turned out, putting Yalding in was the best thing that happened to us all afternoon — even if it was them who made the decision. Their innings folded for a modest 58 off 26 overs, with Alan Wilkes in irresistible form, snaring 6-33 to bring up a thoroughly deserved five-for (and, we're reliably informed, a leg-up to 24th on the club's all-time wicket-takers list, past Robert Muir). Michael Morton offered superb support with 4-11 and six miserly maidens — the sort of spell that gives a batsman nothing but time to reflect on his shortcomings. F Hooker and H Davidson departed without so much as a run to their names, which even by our standards is a chastening way to begin an innings.

With the ball still swinging kindly for us, one might have expected a straightforward stroll to victory. Cricket, mercifully or otherwise, rarely obliges. We posted a healthy 135/3 from our 24 overs, William Buck leading the way with a fine 44, Basil Sears chipping in with 30, and I added an unbeaten 21 for good measure. Bob Arnold had a torrid time behind the stumps, 12 byes escaping his gloves like naughty schoolboys at half-term.

And yet, somehow, we contrived to lose by seven wickets. Quite how remains a mystery best discussed over a pint in the pavilion. We'll be better for it next week — we rather have to be.

🗓️ 05 Sep – 11 Sep 1953 · 1953 — 2 matches

Sun 06 Sep 1953 🧢 1st XIv GK Gillingham CC, Kent · HomeformDLWLW

Lost the toss on Sunday, which these days feels less like bad luck and more like a formality — Gillingham CC duly took first use of a green-ish Linton Park track and made themselves at home. G Veroest anchored things nicely with an unbeaten 32, well supported by A Tansley's 29 and O Barden's 25, and by tea they'd posted a stiff 210 for 9 off 43.1 overs. Only Alan Wilkes seemed to have read the pitch report properly, though — a superb 5 for 57, complete with four maidens, the kind of spell that makes a captain's job embarrassingly easy. Stan Wratten and Tony Snell chipped in with a wicket apiece to keep the runs honest.

Chasing 211 was always going to ask questions of us, and we answered most of them wrong. William Buck played the innings of the day with a fine 43, Stan Wratten added a useful 27, and I contributed 20 myself, though I'll admit it felt more like survival than stroke-play. Sadly Richard Harris and Gordon Fenner both walked the lonely walk for nought — the Duck Club ledger duly updated — and behind the stumps Tony Levick had one of those afternoons, seventeen byes slipping past him like they had a train to catch. We closed on 129 for 8 off 36, well short but not disgraced.

A draw, then — honours shared, pride mostly intact. With Buck now sitting proudly atop the season's run charts and Wilkes leading the wickets, there's clearly enough talent in this side to trouble anyone. We just need to stop giving it away in fours and byes.

Sat 05 Sep 1953 🧢 1st XIv YC Yalding CC · AwayformDDLWL

Saturday last took us down the lanes to Yalding CC, and for once fortune began well: I won the toss and, with what I like to think was strategic acumen rather than sheer optimism, put us in to bat. Michael Morton led the way with a fine 30, D Simmons chipped in with 27, and Alan Wilkes finished unbeaten on 22 as we compiled 157 all out in 36.4 overs — though I must confess my own contribution was rather less illustrious, departing without troubling the scorers, keeping J Barclay company in the pavilion on nought.

If our batting had its wobbles, our bowling had none. Yalding's reply lasted a mere 19.4 overs before they were dismissed for 56, with a. Lewis and R. Latter both sent back for ducks of their own. Gerald Wright was quite unplayable, spinning his way to a magnificent 6 for 31 with three maidens thrown in for good measure, and Alan Wilkes offered fine support with 3 for 21.

There was extra cause for celebration in Gerald's figures: those six wickets carried him past Tony Snell and up to eighteenth on the club's all-time wicket-takers list — a milestone worth raising a glass to. Elsewhere on the season's charts, Basil Sears sits fifth among the 1953 first XI's run-scorers with 264, while Gerald himself leads the wicket column with 31.

A rout, then, by any reasonable reading of a 157 to 56 scoreline — the sort of afternoon that makes the drive to Yalding worthwhile. Heads up, and back to the nets we go, all the same.

🗓️ 29 Aug – 04 Sep 1953 · 1953 — 1 match

Sat 29 Aug 1953 🧢 1st XIv HC Hunton Wanderers CC · AwayformLDDLW

Saturday dawned fair at Hunton Wanderers, though the coin did not smile on us — I called wrong, and we were put into the field. It proved a long afternoon. Pilcher settled in for a fine 65, well supported by Reg Oldfield's 47, and between them they wore down all but our sturdiest bowling. Alan Wilkes toiled manfully for 4-68, J Barclay chipped in with 3-48, and Gerald Wright picked up 1-17 — though R Murray, at least, departed for nought, which offered some small consolation.

Our reply was, in a word, brief. Thirty-one all out in 16.4 overs is not the stuff of Wisden, and the ledger makes for grim reading: N Simmons and Bob Arnold both walked back for ducks, and I confess I joined them — the least said about my own contribution the better. Eleven byes told their own tale of a testing afternoon behind the stumps for William Blight.

There was one gleam amid the gloom: Gerald Wright's solitary wicket nudged him past W H Fryer into 22nd on the club's all-time list — a fine achievement met, I fear, with rather muted applause from a side otherwise licking its wounds. He sits third in this season's wicket charts with 25, while Basil Sears holds fifth in the run charts with 243.

A hundred and forty runs is a chastening margin by anyone's reckoning. Not our finest hour — heads up, and back to the nets.

🗓️ 22 Aug – 28 Aug 1953 · 1953 — 1 match

Sat 22 Aug 1953 🧢 1st XIv CA Charles Arkcoll · HomeformWLDDL

Skipper's Log — Saturday 22 August 1953

Losing the toss against Charles Arkcoll and being invited to field is not, on paper, the tonic a captain dreams of on a warm August Saturday, but the lads set about it with more enthusiasm than my pre-match team talk probably deserved. Wratten and Ames got their innings going nicely for the visitors, 38 and 24 respectively, with Fenner chipping in 21, and for a while it looked as though we might be chasing leather all afternoon. But Alan Wilkes had other ideas, steaming in for a quite magnificent 7-60 with five maidens thrown in for good measure — the sort of spell that has opposition batsmen checking the fixture list for our next visit with some dread. I managed a wicket myself for 28, Gerald Wright picked up another for 40, and between us we saw Morris and Beale both back in the pavilion without troubling the scorer. Arkcoll were shepherded out for 139.

Chasing 140, William Blight batted with real substance for 42, well supported by Richard Harris's 31 — a knock that, pleasingly, carries Harris past Maurice Fenner and up to 12th on the club's all-time run charts. I added 17 down the order, and my one wicket earlier had already nudged me past Ernest Cruttenden into 8th among the club's leading wicket-takers, which felt like a fair trade for the grey hairs. Sadly J Barclay's afternoon ended for nought, a walk of shame we've all taken.

We were bowled out for 158 from 39 overs — enough, gloriously, to get the job done. A fine win, deservedly earned in the field. Onwards.

Not our day, but the nets beckon, and the season charts — Harris third with 402 runs, Fenner leading with 30 wickets — remind us there's still a summer left to play with.

🗓️ 08 Aug – 14 Aug 1953 · 1953 — 2 matches

Sun 09 Aug 1953 🧢 1st XIv AC Anchorians CC · HomeformWDDWL

I called correctly and, with a good drying breeze and a firm-looking track, never seriously considered doing anything but batting first. Two hundred and six all out in exactly 50.1 overs felt about right for the surface — William Buck the architect with a really fine 85, striking the ball with the sort of clarity the rest of us can only admire from the non-striker's end, well supported by Richard Harris (32) and Stan Wratten (30) chipping in around him. A total we felt we could defend, if not quite comfortably.

And defend it we very nearly did. Anchorians dug in through Derek Ufton (47) and A Hart (41), with D Wren (25) keeping the innings ticking, but the middle order rather came and went — M- Baynes and Alan Dixon both back in the hutch without so much as a squeak off the bat, poor devils. The story of the day, though, was John Ames, relentless and miserly in equal measure: 6 for 65 with five maidens, a five-for thoroughly earned and richly deserved, with Michael Morton chipping in a tidy 1 for 22 in support. Tony Levick had a rather more chastening afternoon behind the stumps, 11 byes going begging — these things happen to the best of us.

Nice to note the milestones too: Richard Harris nudging past F S W Cornwallis into 13th on the all-time run charts, and John Ames leapfrogging H Freed into 16th among the club's wicket-takers — the old names shuffling aside for the new.

In the end honours were shared, a draw that satisfied nobody and disappointed nobody either — which, in truth, is often the fairest result cricket has to offer.

Mon 03 Aug 1953 🧢 1st XIv LX Lord Cornwallis XI · HomeformDDWDD

Monday the 3rd of August, and Lord Cornwallis XI rolled up as our visitors. I lost the toss - no change there - and they chose to bat, leaving us first use of the ball on what looked a fair morning for it.

Marsham and Synce departed without troubling the scorers, both sent back by an irresistible Gordon Fenner, who spun his way to 5 for 34 - the pick of a very tidy attack. Tony Tipples held firm for a well-made unbeaten fifty, 51 not out, ably supported by Maurice Fenner's 34, a knock that lifts him to 12th on the club's all-time run-scorers list. As for the skipper - a golden duck. A captain ought to lead from the front; on this occasion I led from somewhere near the back of the queue.

Not the prettiest cricket Linton Park has played, but we got there in the end, and a win by the width of a single wicket is still a win. Heads up, back to the nets.

Special mention, as ever, for the Duck Club: poor J Barclay walked back for nought, quacking all the way. Honours shared, then — a draw earned by application rather than brilliance, and I'll happily take it.

Saturday, 25th July 1953 — Linton Park at home to Dummy Site, and I lost the toss for the umpteenth time this season, which meant we were out in the field while the tea urn stayed cold a while longer.
