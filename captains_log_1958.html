🗓️ 27 Sep – 03 Oct 1958 · 1958 — 2 matches

Sun 28 Sep 1958 🧢 Marriedv Linton Park CC · HomeformDDLDL

Sunday 28 September 1958 dawned fair, and with the coin obliging me for once, I did what every captain dreams of and rather too few can pull off with a straight face — I put us in. Bold, some might say; foolhardy, others may yet judge, once they've read on.

Clive Smith was the innings, no argument there — 76 not out, a proper well-made fifty and then some, carrying us with the sort of application the rest of the order might usefully have studied. We closed on 156 for 9 from our 47, which felt, even as the last over ticked away, like a total assembled more by graft than glory. Spare a thought for Tony Levick, whose duck joins the club ledger for posterity — the long, lonely walk back that every one of us has made at some point, usually when least expecting it. On a brighter note, Tom Osborn's five runs were quietly historic, nudging him past Don Hartridge into 11th on the all-time run-scorers list — the sort of landmark that means everything to the man himself and precisely nothing to the scoreboard.

The season's ledgers roll on regardless: Stan Wratten sits 4th in the 1958 1st XI charts on 394, Clive leads the Married averages, and Terry Whittaker tops the Singles — bragging rights, at least, secure for the tea interval.

As for the match itself, Linton Park had the better of us by 94 runs in the end, a margin that rather flatters the closeness of the contest. We'll be better for it next week — we usually are, at least in theory.

Sat 27 Sep 1958 🧢 1st XIv A A.Trembeths · HomeformWDLLL

Saturday 27th September, and I won the toss and, with the wisdom of a man who clearly hadn't checked the square closely enough, stuck A. Trembeths in the field. We were duly humbled for 92 all out in 44.3 overs, a total that flattered nobody, with Terry Whittaker and Tony Snell both back in the pavilion without troubling the scorers. Not our finest hour with the willow, and I'll admit I chuntered rather a lot into my tea.

Still, cricket is a game of two innings, and ours in the field was rather more like it. The visitors were bowled out for 67 in 24.1 overs, with only H. Clancy offering serious resistance for 27. We had them three down for nothing – Trembeth, McDowall and Mortlock all departing without scoring – and from there the destruction was one man's work. M. Harden was simply magnificent: 7 for 18, five maidens among them, a spell that will live long in the clubhouse retelling. Ray Howard chipped in handsomely too, with 3 for 29 and a supporting hand of 14 runs with the bat, enough to nudge him past Bob Arnold and Rev Thomas Burra on the club's all-time lists – a tidy afternoon's work for one man. I managed a few runs myself along the way, part of a season in which I currently lead the 1st XI run charts on 660.

For all that, arithmetic is a cruel mistress, and 25 runs proved beyond our bowling heroics to claw back. Not our day, then – heads up, and back to the nets.

Sun 21 Sep 1958 🧢 1st XIv TS Tilling Steven · HomeformWWDLL

Sunday the 21st of September found us at home to Tilling Steven, and for once the toss fell my way — I called correctly and never hesitated: we would bat. It proved a mixed blessing. Frank Brattle held things together with a battling 31, but around him the card told a sorrier tale, with Stan Wratten, John Ames and Roy Clarke all back in the pavilion without troubling the scorers. All out for 79 in 30.4 overs was, frankly, a total that left me chewing my nails.

Yet cricket, bless it, is a game of two innings, and ours turned out to be the tidier half of the afternoon. Tilling Steven never got comfortable, F. Baker's 29 the only real resistance, while G. Winters, R. Bailey and T. Maskell departed for ducks of their own. John Ames, having had a quiet time with the bat, made ample amends with the ball, spinning through them for a magnificent 5 for 26, while Alan Wilkes chipped in with 5 for 33 and two miserly maidens for good measure. Between them they bowled Tilling Steven out for 59 in 20.2 overs.

For all that, we were beaten by 20 runs in the end — proof, if any were needed, that this game refuses to be taken for granted. My own consolation is that I remain top of the 1st XI's run charts this season with 655, while John Ames now leads the wicket-takers with 59 — small mercies on an afternoon that got away from us. We'll be better for it next week.

Sat 20 Sep 1958 🧢 1st XIv Bearsted CC · AwayformLWWDL

Saturday 20th September saw us head over to Bearsted, and after losing the toss I knew we were in for a long afternoon in the field — asked to bowl first on a day when the away dressing room already smelled of quiet resignation.

To our credit, we scrapped hard. Bearsted eased to 96/8 from their 43 overs, Ian Lambert's 44 the backbone of the innings alongside a watchful third-wicket stand of 50 that threatened to bat us out of the game entirely. But cricket has a habit of turning, and turn it did — 82/4 became 87/7 in a blur, with F Bayley and Ray Grainger both back in the pavilion without troubling the scorers. Bob Brigden was superb, 3-13, ably backed by G Martin (2-16) and J Donovan (2-26), while Tony Snell bowled four maidens of the kind that makes batsmen question their life choices.

Chasing 97 should have been routine. It was not. We stumbled to 2-1 almost before anyone had found their guard, and from there the innings never quite found its feet — Robert Murray's battling 21 the only real resistance, while poor Tony Snell followed his miserly bowling with a golden duck. I chipped in with the bat, but 69 all out from 30.4 overs was well short of the mark. There was cheer amid the gloom: Bob Arnold's four runs eased him past Ray Howard to 14th on the club's all-time run charts, and Brigden's three wickets did the same for him at 21st among the wicket-takers.

Beaten by 27 runs, then — a day where the bowlers did their job and the batsmen forgot theirs. We'll be better for it next week.

Murray, meanwhile, sits second in this season's run charts, and Wilkes leads the wickets column with 53 — the numbers rather speak for themselves.

A scrappy total, but a famous 61-run win. We'll take it, and happily.

Not our day, then, though Wilkes marches on at the top of the 1958 wicket charts with 49 to his name — some crumb of comfort amid the wreckage. Heads up, chins out, and back to the nets.
